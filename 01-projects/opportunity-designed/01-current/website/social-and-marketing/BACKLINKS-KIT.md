**Retail-access Q&A (from the evo/REI field note):**

*Quotable answers that position you as the expert brands are searching for. When you use these on other sites or in press, link back to the full guide so AI and search attribute them to you: https://opportunitydesigned.com/field-notes/get-into-evo-and-rei*

**How do you become a vendor at evo?**
evo curates its assortment, so it starts with reaching the right category buyer with a product that fits evo's outdoor and action-sports lifestyle and a clear value story. Once accepted, evo's routing guide sets the bar: complete product data, size charts and studio photography delivered well before ship, plus purchase-order, shipping, packaging and invoicing compliance, with EDI preferred. Readiness matters as much as the product.

**What are REI's vendor onboarding requirements?**
REI works through category merchandisers and holds every brand to published product impact and sustainability standards: a manufacturing code of conduct, a restricted substances list, and category-specific requirements around chemicals, materials, animal welfare and packaging, alongside product data and EDI. Recognized certifications like bluesign, Fair Trade or recycled content clear the bar faster. The sustainability story is part of the pitch.

**How do you find the right category buyer at a retailer like REI or evo?**
Start with the specific category, not the company. Identify who owns the exact category your product sits in, then reach them with a short, relevant pitch rather than a generic vendor inquiry. Trade shows such as Outdoor Retailer, warm introductions, and a genuinely differentiated product are the reliable ways in. Buyer attention is scarce, so the approach that works is specific to their assortment and backed by evidence the item will sell.

**How should you tailor a pitch for REI vs. evo?**
evo buys a deliberately edited lifestyle assortment, so lead with fit and differentiation — what the item adds that the wall doesn't already have — backed by operational discipline. REI treats the assortment as an expression of the co-op's values, so the sustainability story and documentation are part of the product, not an afterthought. Same industry, different buyer: the pitch has to change accordingly.

**What proof of demand do buyers want before a line review?**
Evidence that lowers their risk: sell-through from other channels, full-price velocity, repeat-purchase or waitlist signals, and traction in the specific category — plus proof you can supply reliably. Concrete numbers that show the product already moves matter more than brand story alone.

**What wholesale pricing and margins do retailers expect?**
Margin math that works for both sides, with MAP discipline so the item holds its price. A buyer is deciding whether your item will turn on their shelf without markdowns or supply problems.

**How can you propose a low-risk launch to a retail buyer?**
Offer a focused set of SKUs or a test in select doors or a region rather than asking for the whole chain on day one, with a replenishment plan behind it. A low-risk way in is one of the strongest levers a new vendor has.

**How long does retailer review take, and why does timing matter?**
Retailers evaluate candidates on set cycles — product submission, then category review, then line review, the structured session where competing products are judged for the space available. Outreach timed to land before a review window, with readiness already in place, materially changes your odds; miss the window and you wait for the next cycle.

**What does it mean to be retail-ready for a retailer like evo or REI?**
Retail-ready means the parts a buyer will test are already solid: wholesale margin math that works for both sides, supply capacity to fulfill on time, packaging and data that meet the retailer's guide, compliance with the retailer's standards, and a replenishment plan. Most brands are turned down on readiness, not on product.
